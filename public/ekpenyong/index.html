A Royal Tribute

Dr Bassey Ekpenyong

Associate Professor · University of Calabar

60

To my friend, my brother and my counsellor,

There are men whose worth is measured by the offices they hold, and there are men whose worth is measured by what would have been lost had they never lived. Bassey Ekpenyong belongs to the second and rarer order. Today he turns sixty, and I find that the ordinary vocabulary of congratulation will not stretch far enough to carry what I owe him.

Let it be said plainly, and let it be said first: I am alive to write this because of him. There was an occasion — he knows it, and I shall not parade it — when my life stood upon a narrow edge, and it was his hand that drew me back from it. Nations raise monuments for less. I have no monument to offer him. I have only the truth, set down in my own hand, that the Eniong throne in the Efik Kingdom has an occupant today because a scholar from Calabar did what had to be done when it had to be done.

The Peacemaker

Nor was that the only hour in which he stood between me and ruin. There came a season when the throne itself was under siege — when settled things were unsettled, when quiet arrangements were made against us, and when the storm that gathered was of a kind that has broken older houses than mine.

Into that breach walked Dr Ekpenyong. Not with noise. Not with the trumpet of the partisan. He went where the danger was, sat with those who had to be sat with, and spoke the words that had to be spoken — patiently, repeatedly, and at no small cost to his own peace. He carried messages that lesser men would not have touched. He absorbed heat that was never meant for him. And by degrees he turned a confrontation into a conversation, and a conversation into a settlement.

The world remembers the wars that were fought. It rarely learns the names of those who prevented them.

That is the peculiar injustice of peacemaking: its greatest successes leave no evidence. There is no ruin to point to, no wound to display, no headline to preserve. Only a throne still standing, and a Queen still upon it, and a quiet man who never once asked to be thanked in public. So let this stand as the public thanks he never sought.

The Friend, the Brother, the Adviser

Crisis reveals a man; the long ordinary years prove him. In all the time I have known him, Bassey Ekpenyong has been the same person in the small hours as in the great ones — the friend who answers, the brother who tells the truth when the truth is unwelcome, the counsellor whose judgement is worth more than a chamber full of flatterers.

A monarch is surrounded by voices. Most of them wish to be heard; very few wish to be useful. He was always the second kind. He never told me what I wished to hear when it differed from what I needed to hear, and he never once used what he knew of me for his own advancement. In the world I inhabit, that is not merely a virtue. It is nearly a miracle.

And all of this from a man whose life's proper work has been letters — syntax, stylistics, the anatomy of language — who has spent his years at the University of Calabar teaching the young of Cross River how to think clearly and say precisely what they mean. It is fitting. Only a man who has laboured that long over the exact weight of a word could have found, in the hardest of hours, the exact words that were needed.

At Sixty

Sixty years. Three of those decades given to the lecture room and the page. A generation of Nigerians who reason more sharply because he taught them. A family who know him as we cannot. And, folded quietly inside that ordinary academic life, at least two occasions on which he changed the course of another person’s history and told almost no one.

Among our people we do not say that a man of sixty has grown old. We say that he has ripened — that he has become one of those to whom the young are sent for answers. He arrived at that station long before this birthday. Today the calendar merely catches up with what those of us who know him settled long ago.

May the years ahead repay you in the coin you have spent so freely on others: in loyalty, in protection, in friends who arrive before they are summoned.

May your household be a place of peace, and may every kindness you have done in the dark be returned to your children in the light.

May your name be spoken with honour in Calabar, in Cross River, and wherever the Efik Kingdom is named.

And may you be granted many more years yet — in strength, in gladness, and in the full and unhurried enjoyment of a life that has already justified itself many times over.

Happy sixtieth birthday, my brother. The throne you helped to save salutes you.

With enduring affection, gratitude and royal esteem,

Her Royal Majesty Queen Aruk II

Obonganwan of the Efik Kingdom · Cross River, Nigeria
